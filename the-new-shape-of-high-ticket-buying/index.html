The New Shape of High-Ticket Buying

Car buyers who used AI during their search trusted the dealer more, not less. That single finding changes what an AI sales agent is actually for.

Prashant Ramani
August 2026
9 min read

Most high-ticket retail strategy assumes a big purchase has to be a long ordeal. Weeks of research, endless comparison, a buyer who arrives exhausted and suspicious.

Cox Automotive has tracked car buying for sixteen years. In their 2025 study, released this January, the total time spent buying a vehicle went down and satisfaction went up. 76% of new vehicle buyers were highly satisfied, the highest figure Cox has ever recorded. They credit efficiency directly: almost two fewer hours online, around thirty fewer minutes at the dealership.

Less time, more satisfaction, in a year when 62% of consumers said owning a car was becoming too expensive.

So the journey is compressing. It is not compressing evenly, and where the time still goes is the whole story.

Half of it happens before you exist

Cox breaks the total down. Seven hours and eleven minutes researching and shopping online, alone, before any conversation with a seller. Forty six minutes talking to other people. Two hours fifty five visiting dealerships, and another two hours fifty five with the one they finally bought from.

Roughly half the purchase is a solitary research problem that resolves before you know the buyer exists. Most brands understand that in principle. What almost nobody has priced correctly is what happens when an AI turns up inside those seven hours.

The AI was not in the room during that negotiation. It changed how buyers felt about it anyway. They walked in already knowing what a fair number looked like, so they could recognise one when it was offered.

The machine's job is not to close. It is to make the buyer sure, so the human can.

That inverts how this technology usually gets sold. The pitch is substitution, the machine doing what a person used to do. What the data actually describes is a handover.

How much they will actually hand over

Accenture put this to 25,590 people across sixteen countries. Nearly three in four said they would trust a personal AI agent more than their best friend to make a purchase on their behalf. Then they asked what people would let the agent actually do.

Trusted more than a best friend, and fewer than one in ten will let it press the button. They want an advisor, not a proxy.

Accenture's read on where that line sits is the sharpest thing in their report. It is not drawn by how complicated a purchase is. It is drawn by how much the purchase means. Identity, relationships, self-expression. People delegate what feels like work and keep hold of what feels like them.

Which locates high-ticket precisely. A car is identity. So is the sofa in the room where you host people, and the laptop you open every morning. These are not complicated purchases so much as meaningful ones, and meaning is the thing people refuse to hand over. Build toward autonomous checkout in a category like this and you are building for the 9%, in a world where Accenture is blunt that payments remain the wall.

The friction moved

Cox also asked which steps buyers would prefer to complete online, then measured what they actually did. Discovery is largely solved. The four widest gaps in the entire journey are all in the money: selecting finance and insurance products, finalising the price, applying for credit, and being told whether the credit came through.

That is the commitment moment. Buyers are asking to handle the most anxious part of the transaction in private, in their own time, and being walked to a desk instead.

The same tension shows in the shape of the purchase. 63% want a mix of online and in person. 40% get it. Over half buy entirely in person and 7% buy entirely online. That is not preference. That is a process that will not bend.

Two jobs, not one funnel

Put all of that together and the journey splits into two halves that behave nothing like each other.

The first is reaching a decision. Long, solitary, mostly digital, about half the total time, and now often done with an AI. The second is acting on it. Short, anxious, heavy with meaning, still stubbornly human, and starved of the online tools buyers keep asking for.

Almost every strategy I see funds the first and assumes the second will look after itself.

For now, consumers are asking brands to take this on. Accenture found that weekly generative AI users are more than twice as likely to want a retailer or brand as their agent of choice than an AI-native platform. That preference is not guaranteed to hold.

Because the same research found that 37% of behaviourally loyal shoppers would let an agent move them off a brand they prefer if it finds a better fit. Loyalty is becoming conditional on being demonstrably the right answer rather than the familiar one.

What to measure instead

The usual question about an AI sales agent is how much of the sale it can take over. In high-ticket the answer is very little, and it is not going to change soon.

The better question is what condition the buyer is in when they arrive. Cox's AI users turned up more satisfied, more convinced the price was fair, and more likely to say the experience beat their last one. Nothing about the dealership changed between those two groups. The buyer did.

So the number worth watching is not how many steps the agent completed. It is how certain the person was by the time a human took over.

It is early. Only 12% of car buyers visited an AI site last year, rising to 17% among new vehicle buyers. But when Cox asked consumers about the longer horizon, 83% said technologies like AI would change how they buy a vehicle over the next ten years. The direction looks settled. Timing is the only open question.
